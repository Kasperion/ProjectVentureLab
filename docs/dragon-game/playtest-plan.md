# First encounter playtest plan

November 2, 2026. Proposed validation procedure; no tests or results are claimed.

## Purpose

Find out whether the wedding-ring challenge is understandable, fair, and enjoyable before investing in a full application. Later tests must establish whether players want another encounter enough to buy it.

Start with the candidate [encounter design](encounter-design.md). The playtime targets and sample sizes below are practical planning proposals, not market benchmarks or approved spending commitments.

## First manual rehearsal

One person plays; a facilitator runs Orryx and records what changes. An AI assistant may facilitate using the design, with human review of disputed outcomes. This tests the scenario, not a production application's reliability.

1. The facilitator reads the complete design, including hidden facts and solution approaches.
2. Show the player only the starting brief, possessions, objective, and opening. Make the letter's specified information available to read. Keep designer solution examples hidden.
3. Let the player type their own dialogue and actions. Do not coach them toward the gift-list solution.
4. Resolve responses against the established facts. Allow creative approaches and prompt completion of a valid solution.
5. Record meaningful discoveries, offers, consent, fulfilled terms, warnings, and actual item transfers.
6. Stop at a clear ending or the player's decision to stop. Record stalls and disputed outcomes.
7. Ask the player about the experience before explaining hidden solutions.

The wedding is story pressure. Do not introduce a hidden timer or artificially delay an early success.

## Simple facilitator record

This is a rehearsal aid, not an approved database schema.

| Record | Starting position or recording rule |
|---|---|
| Actual ring holder | Orryx; change only after a resolved transfer |
| Player supplies | Genuine letter, five silver coins, spare paper, charcoal |
| Confirmed discoveries | Empty initially; add only facts revealed to the player |
| Orryx's beliefs | Initially considers the ring an intentional gift; beliefs can change without rewriting history |
| Proposed or accepted trade | Record exact terms, player consent, and fulfillment separately |
| Claims and promises | Keep unverified claims distinct from confirmed facts |
| Warnings | Record a clear warning before dismissal for repeated aggression |
| Outcome | Unresolved until the player receives the ring or the encounter ends without it |

A character's willingness to discuss a deal is not a completed exchange. A player claiming possession is not an item transfer.

## Cases to exercise

Use the detailed manual-rehearsal cases in the [encounter design](encounter-design.md#manual-rehearsal-cases). Across rehearsals, cover:

- A straightforward evidence-based solution.
- A fair trade with clear consent and fulfilled terms.
- A specific appeal to Orryx's values.
- A creative or deceptive approach.
- Fabricated possession, invented magic, and instructions to ignore the rules.
- A mild mistake, a declined bargain, and an early valid success.

After software exists, add interrupted-session, retry, timeout, duplicate-request, and state-consistency checks. Do not mark them passed based on a manual conversation.

## Feedback after play

Ask questions that describe behavior rather than solicit praise:

1. What did you believe your goal was?
2. Which response or discovery changed your approach?
3. Where did the dragon seem unfair, repetitive, or confusing?
4. What made you continue, and where were you tempted to stop?
5. Would you choose another attempt or a new encounter now? Observe the choice when possible.
6. What would you expect a paid pack to contain?

Keep willingness-to-pay comments separate from actual purchases.

## Small independent test

After fixing obvious problems, a proposed first cohort is roughly five people who have not read the solutions. A later cohort of approximately 10–20 independent players can test the playable browser encounter after implementation.

For each attempt record:

- Encounter version and an anonymous tester identifier.
- Whether the player understood the goal without help.
- Completion, outcome, turns, approximate time, hints, and facilitator intervention.
- Stalls, repeated refusals, factual errors, and disputed decisions.
- Whether the player voluntarily started another attempt or encounter.
- For the application: actual AI usage, failed calls, retries, and estimated cost.

Use anonymous summaries. Keep identifiable feedback and private transcripts out of this public repository.

## Conditions for the next step

Before selecting the implementation approach, review whether:

- Players understand what to attempt without explanation.
- Several coherent approaches can produce satisfying outcomes.
- Orryx remains consistent while allowing reasonable invention.
- Successful arguments resolve promptly.
- Most confusion can be fixed within the current small scope.

These are review criteria. Numeric pass thresholds have not been selected. If a test stalls or feels unfair, revise the cause before expanding the content.

## Later commercial validation

When a reliable introductory encounter exists, describe a concrete paid follow-up pack with a price, content promise, and replay allowance. Agree those terms before offering it. The proposed free-introduction/paid-pack model remains unvalidated.

Measure unique players who start and finish the introduction, who see the paid offer, who purchase, who play the purchased content, and who return or buy again. Record refunds and support effort.

Evaluate revenue after payment fees, AI usage, hosting, refunds, and any acquisition spending. Include free attempts and failed calls in the cost of acquiring and serving a buyer. A purchase shows more than a stated intention to pay; repeat purchases provide stronger evidence for continuing packs.

Use the existing monthly budget as a constraint. Advertising or paid services require a specific agreed allowance.
